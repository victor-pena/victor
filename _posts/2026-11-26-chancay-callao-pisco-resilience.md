The recent update of Peru’s macroeconomic projections towards 2031 by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MEF)[^1], together with the Central Reserve Bank’s decision to raise its reference interest rate to 4.5%[^2], highlights an increasingly relevant issue: the capacity of territorial systems to absorb climatic, logistical and geopolitical disruptions.

While the MEF anticipates sustained economic growth driven by private investment in mining and infrastructure, inflationary pressures associated with El Niño, energy prices and international geopolitical tensions reveal the vulnerability of the material systems that sustain economic activity.

Monetary policy can moderate domestic demand and contain inflationary expectations. Fiscal policy can mobilize resources for reconstruction and infrastructure investment. Neither, however, can immediately reorganize the territorial conditions through which external shocks propagate across the economy.

In this context, the emergence of Chancay, Callao and Pisco as differentiated logistics nodes offers an opportunity to rethink the spatial organization of Peru’s economy.

Chancay provides direct connectivity with Asian markets and opportunities for integrating Peru into transpacific supply chains. Callao concentrates established commercial, industrial and metropolitan logistics functions. Pisco combines port infrastructure, energy facilities, agro-industrial production and potential connectivity with southern mining territories.

The ports of Chancay, Callao and Pisco are distributed along approximately 300 km of Peru’s central coastal corridor. Chancay lies about 80 km north of Callao, while Pisco’s General San Martín terminal is approximately 260 km south by road.

This relative proximity creates opportunities for functional complementarity, cargo redistribution and coordinated logistics operations, while providing a degree of geographical redundancy against localized disruption.

Their strategic value lies not exclusively in their individual competitiveness but in their potential functional complementarity.

Traditional logistics planning prioritizes efficiency, specialization and cost reduction. However, an increasingly uncertain international environment demands additional criteria: geographical diversification, redundancy, adaptability and continuity of economic flows. Infrastructure capacity that appears redundant under normal conditions may become indispensable during periods of disruption.

The development of a polycentric logistics system could reduce dependence on individual nodes, facilitate the redistribution of cargo and mitigate the economic consequences of infrastructure failures, transport interruptions or geopolitical conflicts.

Nevertheless, the existence of three ports does not automatically constitute a territorial system. Such integration requires complementary infrastructure, inland connectivity, institutional coordination and mechanisms for managing shared vulnerabilities. It also demands the capacity to govern the urban expansion, informal land markets and environmental pressures generated by capital accumulation around these nodes.

This perspective introduces a broader understanding of territorial planning. Rather than being restricted to land-use regulation, territorial planning becomes an instrument for organizing the material conditions of macroeconomic stability.

Peru’s challenge towards 2031 is therefore not simply to increase port capacity or attract greater investment. It is to transform these investments into an articulated territorial system capable of sustaining economic flows under adverse conditions.

**Macroeconomic resilience is also a spatially produced capacity.**
