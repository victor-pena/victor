As President José Antonio Kast travels to Asia for the APEC summit, Chile faces a growing contradiction between its international economic ambitions and the declining legitimacy of its domestic political order. The recent *bencinazo*, truckers' protests, and controversies surrounding the justice minister have exposed the fragility of a government elected on promises of security, prosperity, and restored authority.

Yet Chile's crisis extends beyond Kast's administrative failures. Its origins lie in the political-economic order established after the Pinochet dictatorship.

The democratic transition restored electoral competition without fundamentally transforming the concentration of economic power. Major financial-industrial groups, including those associated with the Luksic and Yarur families, retained influential positions in banking, mining, commerce, and investment.

Meanwhile, the internationalization of Chile's political elites encouraged a convergence of governing rationalities across the center-left and right. Fiscal discipline, financial credibility, investor confidence, and global market integration became the shared foundations of public policy.

The institutional left increasingly administered an economic order it had once sought to transform. Political competition continued, but largely within boundaries compatible with the reproduction of concentrated capital.

This arrangement was not simply neoliberal continuity. It constituted a hegemonic settlement in which democratic legitimacy and financial accumulation reinforced one another.

The 2019 uprising revealed its underlying contradiction: economic growth and international credibility had not produced an equally legitimate distribution of wealth, opportunity, and decision-making power.

Subsequent governments have struggled to reconstruct that consensus. Kast's presidency represents an attempt to restore political authority without substantially altering the economic structures whose legitimacy remains contested.

His APEC participation illustrates another paradox. Despite ideological proximity to Washington, Chile's export economy remains deeply connected to China. The interests of internationally integrated capital constrain the geopolitical choices of successive governments.

**Chile's fundamental problem may not be the failure of one government. It could be the declining capacity of a financial-industrial order to reproduce the political consent that sustained it for decades.**

The central question is whether international economic integration can remain politically sustainable without transforming the domestic distribution of power.
