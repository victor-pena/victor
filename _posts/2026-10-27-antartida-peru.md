Chile imagines Antarctica as the ultimate fulfillment of its longitudinal geography. Argentina projects its sovereignty from the South Atlantic toward the Antarctic Peninsula. Peru possesses a different historical geography, it is that of a center capable of organizing relations across South America.

The Viceroyalty of Peru once encompassed territories corresponding to present-day Chile, Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay and Uruguay. **Lima** articulated an extensive system of mining centers, agricultural territories, administrative cities and maritime routes. Its power did not derive from uniform territorial occupation, but from organizing heterogeneous spaces through political and commercial networks.

The Bourbon reforms, followed by independence, fragmented that imperial geography. Yet Peru retained something more enduring than colonial jurisdiction, its position between the Pacific, the Andes and the Amazon.

Today, a different spatial order may be emerging. Chancay, Callao and Pisco constitute the nucleus of a Central Peruvian Pacific Complex, while Matarani and Ilo connect southern Peru with Bolivia and the continental corridors extending toward Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina. Antofagasta could become a multimodal hinge between the South American interior and Peruvian-centered maritime networks.

Unlike the viceroyalty, this emerging system would not depend on territorial possession. Its architecture would be constituted by competing global operators—COSCO, DP World, APM Terminals, MSC and international infrastructure capital—whose coexistence Peru could organize without surrendering control of the system as a whole.

Carl Schmitt's concept of *nomos* helps identify what is at stake: geopolitical power emerges not only from possessing territory, but from establishing the concrete order through which space is appropriated, divided and organized.

Peru need not recover an imperial past, nor imagine a geographical destiny.

**The viceroyalty organized South America through jurisdiction. A contemporary Peruvian nomos could organize it through connectivity, commerce and institutional capacity.**

The strategic question is no longer how much territory Peru possesses, but how much South American space can be articulated through relations centered on Peru.
