South America is usually interpreted through ideologies: left versus right, populism versus liberalism, state versus market. Yet these categories often conceal a more fundamental question:

**How does a society organize power?**

Classical Greece offers two ideal types that remain surprisingly useful.

Athens organized power through commerce, navigation, openness, public debate, and continuous interaction with the outside world. Wealth emerged from exchange. Political life was noisy, unstable and often contradictory, but remarkably creative. Athens constantly absorbed foreigners, merchants, artisans and new ideas. Its strength was movement.

Sparta organized power differently. Stability, discipline, hierarchy and cohesion constituted its political ideal. The objective was not continuous innovation but the preservation of an established order. Military organization, social homogeneity and institutional continuity were valued above commercial dynamism. Its strength was permanence.

No contemporary nation is literally Athens or Sparta. These are ideal types. Nevertheless, they illuminate important differences among South American political cultures.

Peru increasingly resembles an Athenian polity.

Its geography connects the Pacific, the Andes and the Amazon. Lima has become one of the principal logistical gateways of western South America. Informal entrepreneurship flourishes despite institutional weaknesses. Internal migration continuously reshapes society. New commercial corridors emerge. Economic adaptation frequently precedes political reform.

Observers often describe these characteristics as vulnerabilities: fragmentation, instability, informality and governmental turnover.

Perhaps they are also sources of resilience.

A society accustomed to uncertainty develops unusual capacities for adaptation. Political crises rarely paralyze commercial life completely. Markets reorganize. Entrepreneurs discover opportunities. Institutions eventually adjust to social realities rather than the reverse.

Many neighboring countries have followed a different trajectory.

Chile and Uruguay, despite their considerable differences, have historically emphasized institutional continuity, administrative discipline, elite coordination and gradual reform. Stability itself became a national objective. Political moderation acquired symbolic value. Public administration rewarded predictability and consensus.

This model generated undeniable achievements: macroeconomic credibility, orderly public administration and relatively strong institutions.

Yet every political virtue contains the possibility of becoming its own limitation.

When institutional reproduction becomes more important than institutional adaptation, societies begin selecting leaders primarily for their compatibility with the existing order rather than for their capacity to transform it. Stability slowly evolves into path dependence.

Pierre Bourdieu described this process as the **reproduction of the field**[^1]. Political, bureaucratic, academic and media elites reproduce the symbolic capital that legitimizes their own position. Innovation increasingly comes from within the accepted boundaries of the field. Criticism remains permissible provided it does not fundamentally challenge the underlying rules.

The result is a polity optimized for continuity.

Peru presents almost the opposite configuration.

Its fields are comparatively open, conflictive and unstable. Elite circulation is faster. Political parties are weaker. Governments frequently change. This creates enormous costs, but it also reduces institutional closure and permits new actors to emerge.

Athens was frequently chaotic.

Sparta appeared orderly.

History nevertheless reminds us that neither model possessed a monopoly on success.

Athens eventually fell during the Peloponnesian War, aided by Persian financial support for Sparta and by its own strategic overextension. Yet Sparta's victory did not inaugurate a durable civilization. Its supremacy proved remarkably brief. The society that excelled at preserving order struggled to generate the commercial, intellectual and political dynamism necessary for long-term leadership.

The lesson extends beyond antiquity.

The central question for South America is not whether countries should imitate Athens or Sparta.

It is whether their institutions increase the collective capacity to act in a world defined by technological disruption, demographic transition, global logistics and geopolitical competition.

Commercial societies without institutions become fragile.

Institutional societies without adaptation become rigid.

The challenge of the twenty-first century is to build republics capable of combining the openness of Athens with the institutional discipline of Sparta.

That balance, rather than the victory of one model over the other, may determine which societies become the principal organizers of South America's future.

---
[^1] Note
A field is not simply an institution. It is a structured social space—politics, academia, journalism, bureaucracy, business—in which actors compete for authority according to rules that are largely accepted by everyone participating. Each field defines what counts as competence, legitimacy, prestige and success.
